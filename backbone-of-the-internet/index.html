The Internet, Explained

The Backbone of the Internet

How a message travels from New York to Tokyo in less than a second, through radio waves, glass and the floor of the Pacific Ocean.

R
Based on articles by TeleGeography, Amazon Web Services, the Pew Charitable Trusts and ipfyi
Summarised by Rachit Ratnam Mudaliar
Published Nov. 15, 2026 · 10 min read

A student in New York types “Hello!” and presses Send. Less than a second later, a friend in Tokyo reads it on her phone, more than 13,000 kilometres away. It feels like magic, and most people imagine the message flying through the air, perhaps to a satellite and back.

The real answer is much more physical. Most of that journey happens through thin strands of glass: under city streets, across a whole country and, finally, along the floor of the Pacific Ocean. In this article, we follow that one message, step by step. On the way, we meet seven terms that explain how the internet really works.

  1. Internet
  2. Cellular network
  3. Optical fiber
  4. ISP
  5. Internet backbone
  6. Cable landing station
  7. Submarine cable

STOP 1A Network of Networks

Before we follow the message, we need to understand where it travels. The internet is not one giant computer, and no single company owns it. It is millions of separate networks — home Wi-Fi networks, university networks, mobile operators, cloud companies — all connected to each other and all using the same rules to exchange data.

Because these networks agree on how to pass data along, a message can start on one network and finish on another, on the other side of the world.

Key term
Internet
Meaning
A global network of networks that connects billions of devices around the world.
In a sentence
Without the internet, a message from New York could never reach Tokyo in less than a second.

STOP 2From the Phone to the Tower

When the student presses Send, the message leaves the phone without any cable. The phone turns it into radio waves and sends it to the nearest mobile tower, which may be only a few hundred metres away.

Mobile operators divide cities and countries into many small areas called cells. Each cell is served by its own tower. When you walk or travel, your phone moves from one cell to the next, and the network hands your connection over from tower to tower, so your call or message is not interrupted.

Key term
Cellular network
Meaning
A mobile network divided into small areas called cells, each served by its own tower.
In a sentence
Without a cellular network, your phone would never be able to send or receive messages or make calls.

STOP 3The Wireless Part Is Very Short

Here is the first surprise. The wireless part of the journey is only the short distance between the phone and the tower. From the tower onward, the message travels through cables, and those cables are made of glass.

Older networks used copper wires and sent data as electrical signals. Today, long-distance internet traffic travels as light through optical fiber. Light inside glass moves at about 200,000 kilometres per second, loses very little strength over long distances and is not disturbed by electrical interference. One fiber can even carry many colours of light at the same time, each colour with its own stream of data.

Key term
Optical fiber
Meaning
A glass strand about as thin as a human hair that carries data as pulses of light.
In a sentence
Without optical fiber, the internet could never carry so much data so fast over such long distances.

STOP 4The Company That Connects You

The fiber from the tower leads into the network of the student’s mobile operator. This company is her internet service provider. Every message she sends, and every website she opens, passes through its network first.

The provider reads the message’s destination and sees that it must go to Japan. No single provider has cables everywhere, so providers connect with each other at special buildings called internet exchange points, where networks meet and pass traffic directly from one to another.

Key term
ISP (Internet Service Provider)
Meaning
The company that connects your phone or home to the internet.
In a sentence
Without an ISP, your phone would have no connection to the rest of the internet.
The internet is not in the clouds. It is in glass, under our streets and on the ocean floor.

STOP 5Across the Country, Underground

Now the message must cross the United States, from New York to the West Coast. It travels through the internet backbone: bundles of very high-capacity fiber cables buried along roads, railways and pipelines. Powerful machines called core routers sit along the way. Like traffic police at a busy crossing, they read each piece of data and send it in the right direction.

The distance is about 4,000 kilometres. For light in glass, that takes only about 20 milliseconds, less than the blink of an eye. And if one cable is damaged, the routers automatically find another path.

Key term
Internet backbone
Meaning
High-capacity fiber cables, often buried underground, that connect cities and countries.
In a sentence
Buried under roads and railways, the internet backbone carries the message across the USA in about 20 milliseconds.

STOP 6Where the Land Ends

On the Pacific coast, the land network ends. To reach Japan, the message must now go into the sea. The place where this happens is a quiet, secure building near the beach: a cable landing station.

Here, the cable from the ocean comes ashore and is connected to the networks on land. The station also sends electricity into the submarine cable, because the equipment inside the cable on the ocean floor needs power to work.

Key term
Cable landing station
Meaning
A building on the coast where a submarine cable comes out of the sea and connects to networks on land.
In a sentence
At the cable landing station, the message leaves the land network and enters the sea.

STOP 7The Floor of the Pacific Ocean

This is the most surprising part of the journey. The message does not go up to a satellite. It goes down, to the bottom of the ocean. A submarine cable carries it about 9,000 kilometres across the Pacific, lying on the sea floor, sometimes thousands of metres deep.

Near the coast, these cables are protected with thick steel armour against ship anchors and fishing nets. In the deep ocean, a cable is only about as thick as a garden hose. Inside are the optical fibers that carry the light, and along the cable there are devices called repeaters, which make the light signal strong again so it can cross the whole ocean.

According to the International Telecommunication Union, more than 99% of international data travels through submarine cables. TeleGeography, a research company that maps them, counts more than 600 cable systems in service around the world. Satellites are useful for ships, planes and remote areas, but they carry only a small part of international traffic.

Key term
Submarine cable
Meaning
A fiber-optic cable laid on the ocean floor that connects continents.
In a sentence
Without submarine cables, more than 99% of international data would have no way to cross the oceans.

THE FULL CIRCLEThe Same Journey, in Reverse

When the cable reaches Japan, the journey repeats in reverse. The message comes ashore at a cable landing station, travels through the Japanese internet backbone to the friend’s ISP, then through optical fiber to a tower near her home. Finally, radio waves carry it the last few hundred metres to her phone.

In total, the message travels more than 13,000 kilometres. Light in glass covers that distance in less than a tenth of a second. That is why the friend in Tokyo sees “Hello!” almost at the same moment it was sent.

What About Starlink?

You may be asking: what about satellites? Today the best-known satellite internet service is Starlink, run by the American company SpaceX. Instead of a few large satellites far away, Starlink uses thousands of small satellites in low Earth orbit, only a few hundred kilometres above the ground. According to the satellite tracker KeepTrack, more than 11,000 Starlink satellites were in orbit in October 2026.

A Starlink user has a small dish that sends signals up to the nearest satellite. The satellite passes the data to a ground station, and some satellites also pass data to each other using lasers. This makes Starlink very useful where cables cannot reach: on ships and planes, in remote villages and after natural disasters.

But Starlink does not replace the cables. A satellite network can carry only a small fraction of the data that fiber carries, and its ground stations are themselves connected to the same fiber backbone described in this article. For traffic between continents, the ocean floor is still the main road.

Who Owns the Cables?

For most of the twentieth century, international cables were paid for by groups of telephone and telecom companies. Each company paid part of the cost and received a share of the cable’s capacity.

Today, the biggest new investors are technology companies. According to TeleGeography, content providers such as Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon are now major investors in new cables, and its list of cables in which these companies have invested has grown to more than 60. Their services, from video streaming to cloud computing, send so much data between continents that owning cables makes sense for them. Google, for example, fully owns several cables, including Curie, Dunant and Grace Hopper.

Building the cables is a job for only a few specialist companies, such as SubCom in the USA, Alcatel Submarine Networks in France and NEC in Japan. They manufacture the cable and the repeaters, and special cable ships lay them slowly across the ocean floor. Because so much of the world’s communication depends on these cables, governments also treat them as critical infrastructure that must be protected.

Conclusion: The Internet Is Not Just Air

We often imagine the internet as something wireless, floating in the air around us. In reality, the wireless part is only the first and the last few hundred metres of the journey. Everything in between is tonnes of physical infrastructure: cell towers, millions of kilometres of glass fiber, cables under our streets, quiet buildings on the coast, cable ships and hundreds of cables on the ocean floor, built and repaired by engineers and paid for by companies around the world.

So the next time you press Send, remember: your words travel as light, through glass, across the sea.
